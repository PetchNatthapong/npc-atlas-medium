1. Acanthosis Nigricans (AN)

Hyperinsulinemia-Associated Dermatosis
📷
Acanthosis NigricansWikimedia image unavailable (rate-limited)
Acanthosis nigricans — hyperpigmented, velvety plaques in axillaWikimedia Commons / Dermnet (CC Licensed)

Description: Dark, velvety, thickened plaques with raised, hyperpigmented skin. Typically appears in body folds — axillae, neck, groin, and inframammary regions. Caused by hyperinsulinemia stimulating keratinocyte and dermal fibroblast proliferation via IGF-1 receptors.

Prevalence

Type 2 DMHigh Very commonStrong — 8+ studies confirm
Prediabetes / ObesityHigh Very commonStrong association
Type 1 DMModerate Less commonPresent but less studied
General PopulationLow ~7–14%Correlates with insulin resistance
Diabetes Type Association
Type 2 DM — Primary Prediabetes — Strong marker Obesity-linked

Clinical Significance: AN is the strongest skin marker for diabetes/prediabetes screening (8 validated studies). Presence of AN should prompt HbA1c measurement. Also associated with metabolic syndrome, PCOS, and insulin resistance.

  • Key cutaneous marker for prediabetic and diabetic state (systematic review — 8 articles)
  • Axilla and neck are most common sites
  • Can precede overt hyperglycemia by years
  • Malignant AN (paraneoplastic) must be ruled out in non-obese, older adults

2. Diabetic Dermopathy (Shin Spots)

Diabetic Microangiopathic Change
🔴
Diabetic DermopathyNo specific clinical photograph available on Wikimedia Commons
Presents as oval brown atrophic patches on anterior shins
Most common skin manifestation of DM (~30–70%)
'">
Diabetic dermopathy — oval, atrophic, hyperpigmented patches on shinsWikimedia Commons (Public Domain) — no specific clinical photo available

Description: The most common cutaneous manifestation of diabetes mellitus. Presents as multiple, bilateral, oval or round, dull red or brownish atrophic patches with fine scaling, most commonly located on the anterior shins (pretibial areas). Lesions are painless, 0.5–3 cm in diameter, and may heal with scarring. Related to diabetic microangiopathy and neuropathy.

Clinical Significance: Strong predictor of microvascular complications (retinopathy & nephropathy). Lesions reflect underlying vascular damage. 2025 J Clin Med review confirmed consistent association with diabetic microangiopathy.

  • Most common skin finding in long-standing diabetes
  • Consistent association with microvascular complications (retinopathy, nephropathy)
  • Also called "shin spots" — presents bilaterally on shins
  • Predictive marker for cardiovascular and renal complications

3. Necrobiosis Lipoidica (NL)

Necrobiosis Lipoidica Diabeticorum (NLD)
📷
Necrobiosis LipoidicaWikimedia image unavailable (rate-limited)
Necrobiosis lipoidica — yellow-brown atrophic plaques with violaceous borders on legWikimedia Commons / Warfieldian (CC Licensed)

Description: A rare but distinctive granulomatous dermatosis characterized by well-demarcated, yellowish-brown or erythematous plaques with an atrophic, telangiectatic center and raised violaceous borders. Most frequently located on the anterior shins and lower legs. Lesions may be painful, ulcerate, and heal poorly. Affects women more than men (3:1 ratio).

Prevalence
PopulationPrevalenceEvidence

NL patients with DMModerate 60–65%Majority of NL patients are diabetic

Type 1 DM — More common Type 2 DM — Less common Prediabetes — Present

Clinical Significance: NL is strongly associated with macrovascular disease and metabolic syndrome. The presence of NL should prompt cardiovascular risk assessment. Only ~30% of lesions heal spontaneously; treatment is challenging.

  • Well-demarcated plaques with atrophic, telangiectatic yellow-brown center
  • Strong macrovascular disease association (CV risk assessment recommended)
  • Painful lesions with poor healing tendency
  • More common in Type 1 DM (women > men, 3:1 ratio)

4. Bullosis Diabeticorum (Diabetic Blisters)

Bullosis Diabeticorum — Spontaneous Non-Inflammatory Blisters
Bullosis diabeticorum — tense, non-inflammatory bullae on feet and handsBMJ Case Reports (CC Licensed) — casereports.bmj.com/e254182

Description: Rare, spontaneous, non-inflammatory, painless, tense bullae (blisters) appearing on the feet, hands, and lower legs. Lesions are sterile, single or multiple, and range from 0.5–6 cm. They appear suddenly without preceding trauma. Most common in long-standing, poorly controlled Type 2 DM with neuropathy. Heals spontaneously within 2–6 weeks without scarring.

Prevalence
PopulationPrevalenceEvidence
General DM populationLow ~0.5%Rare — primarily case reports
Long-standing T2DMModerate More commonAssociated with neuropathy
Type 1 DMLow Less commonFewer documented cases
Men > WomenModerate 2:1 ratioMale predominance noted
Diabetes Type Association
Type 2 DM — Primary Type 1 DM — Less common Rare condition

Clinical Significance: Bullosis diabeticorum has limited evidence — primarily documented through case reports and case series. However, presence of spontaneous blisters in a diabetic patient should prompt evaluation for neuropathy and glycemic control. Lesions heal spontaneously; care is supportive.

  • Spontaneous, painless, tense bullae — usually on feet and hands
  • Primarily reported in case studies — evidence level is lower
  • Associated with long-standing DM and diabetic neuropathy
  • Heals spontaneously without scarring within 2–6 weeks

5. Scleredema Diabeticorum

Scleredema Adultorum of Buschke — Diabetic Thick Skin
Scleredema adultorum — diffuse, woody skin thickening on neck and upper backBMJ Case Reports (CC Licensed) — casereports.bmj.com/e254182

Description: A rare, chronic condition characterized by painless, symmetrical, diffuse, woody induration and thickening of the skin, typically beginning on the neck and spreading to the face, shoulders, and upper back. Skin is non-pitting, feels tight, and joint mobility may be reduced. Caused by abnormal collagen deposition in the dermis with mucin accumulation.

Clinical Significance: Scleredema correlates with macrovascular disease and metabolic syndrome. The condition causes significant morbidity — joint contractures and reduced mobility can develop. Tight glycemic control is essential; physical therapy helps maintain mobility.

  • Symmetrical, woody, non-pitting skin thickening — neck and upper back
  • Reduces joint mobility — can cause contractures
  • Strong macrovascular disease and metabolic syndrome association
  • More common in long-standing Type 2 DM with obesity

6. Eruptive Xanthomas

Lipid-Disordered Dermatoses — Hypertriglyceridemia Marker
📷
XanthomaWikimedia image unavailable (rate-limited)
Xanthoma — yellowish papules indicating lipid deposition in skinWikimedia Commons (CC Licensed)

Description: Sudden eruption of numerous, small, firm, reddish-yellow or orange papules (1–4mm) on the extensor surfaces of the arms, legs, buttocks, and back. Lesions are pruritic or tender, appear in crops, and represent cutaneous deposits of triglycerides due to severe hypertriglyceridemia. A hallmark of uncontrolled diabetes with lipid disorders.

Prevalence
PopulationPrevalenceEvidence
DM with hypertriglyceridemiaModerate VariableDirectly linked to TG levels >1000mg/dL
Type 2 DMModerate More commonStrongly associated with T2DM
Cardiovascular riskHigh Very HighStrong predictor of severe CV risk
Dyslipidemia markerHigh Very specificIndicates severe hypertriglyceridemia
Diabetes Type Association
Type 2 DM — Primary Severe dyslipidemia marker High CV risk

Clinical Significance: Eruptive xanthomas indicate severe hypertriglyceridemia (typically TG >1000 mg/dL) and represent a major cardiovascular risk marker. Treatment involves aggressive lipid management and glycemic control. Lesions resolve with correction of hypertriglyceridemia.

  • Sudden eruption of reddish-yellow papules — marker of severe hypertriglyceridemia
  • Cardiovascular risk is very high — requires urgent lipid management
  • Lesions resolve when triglycerides are controlled
  • Primarily associated with Type 2 DM and metabolic syndrome

7. Pruritus (Chronic Itching) & Xerosis

Diabetes-Associated Pruritus & Dry Skin
Diabetic pruritus and xerosis — chronic dry, itchy skin on lower legsBMJ Case Reports (CC Licensed) — casereports.bmj.com/e254182

Description: Chronic, persistent itching is one of the most common skin symptoms in diabetes mellitus. It can be generalized or localized (especially to the scalp, feet, and lower legs). Often associated with xerosis (dry skin) due to autonomic neuropathy and impaired sweat gland function. Pruritus can significantly impair quality of life and disturb sleep.

Clinical Significance: Pruritus is often the first symptom that leads diabetic patients to seek medical attention. Persistent pruritus, especially in the anogenital area and feet, should prompt diabetes screening. Xerosis from autonomic neuropathy is a major contributor.

  • Very common symptom (~11–64% of diabetics) — often the first presenting complaint
  • Associated with diabetic autonomic neuropathy and xerosis
  • Localized (scalp, feet, legs) or generalized
  • Quality of life impact is significant — can disturb sleep

8. Skin Tags (Acrochordons)

Fibroepithelial Polyps — Insulin Resistance Marker
📷
Skin Tag (Acrochordon)Wikimedia image unavailable (rate-limited)
Acrochordon (skin tag) — pedunculated, skin-colored polyp on skin surfaceWikimedia Commons / Xheedv (CC Licensed)

Description: Small, soft, pedunculated, skin-colored or hyperpigmented growths (1mm–1cm) that hang from the skin surface. Found on the neck, axillae, eyelids, groin, and inframammary regions. Strongly associated with insulin resistance, obesity, and hyperinsulinemia. They are among the most validated cutaneous markers for diabetes/prediabetes screening.

Clinical Significance: Skin tags are the second-most validated skin marker for DM/prediabetes screening (after acanthosis nigricans). 5+ systematic review articles confirm association. Presence of multiple skin tags — especially in the axillae and neck — should prompt HbA1c measurement.

  • Second-most validated skin marker for DM/prediabetes screening (5 studies)
  • Multiple skin tags — especially axilla/neck — should trigger diabetes screening
  • Strongly correlated with hyperinsulinemia and metabolic syndrome
  • More prevalent in obese individuals — increases with BMI

9. Granuloma Annulare (GA)

Benign Granulomatous Dermatosis — Perforating Variant in DM
📷
Granuloma AnnulareWikimedia image unavailable (rate-limited)
Granuloma annulare — annular papules on dorsal handWikimedia Commons (CC Licensed)

Description: A benign granulomatous skin condition presenting as multiple, pink-red or flesh-colored papules arranged in annular (ring-shaped) plaques, typically on the hands, feet, and dorsal surfaces. In diabetics, a generalized/perforating variant is more commonly seen. The exact pathophysiology linking GA to diabetes is not fully understood.

  • 3 articles confirm association with diabetes (limited but positive evidence)
  • Generalized/perforating variant more linked to DM
  • More studies needed to establish as reliable screening marker

10. Yellow Palms and Soles

Carotenemia & Diabetic Dermopathy — Skin Discoloration
📷
XanthelasmaWikimedia image unavailable (rate-limited)
Yellowish skin deposits — carotenemia indicator also seen on palms/soles in DMWikimedia Commons / Klaus D. Peter (CC Licensed)

Description: Yellow-orange discoloration of the palms, soles, and sometimes the entire skin, caused by carotenemia (elevated beta-carotene levels) due to increased vegetable intake and altered vitamin A metabolism in diabetes. Unlike jaundice, sclerae remain unaffected. Also known as "diabetic xanthochromia." Usually harmless but indicates dietary habits and metabolic disturbances.

11. Rubeosis Faciei Diabeticorum

Facial Redness — Microvascular Dilatation
Rubeosis faciei — persistent facial erythema and telangiectasia in diabetesWikimedia Commons (Public Domain) — representative image

Description: A persistent, bilateral facial flushing or reddening of the face, particularly the cheeks and forehead, caused by microvascular dilatation and increased vascular permeability associated with diabetes. Unlike ordinary blushing, it is persistent and not related to temperature or emotions. Can include telangiectasias and edema. May reflect microvascular dysfunction.

Prevalence
PopulationPrevalenceEvidence
Diabetic patientsModerate Variable1 article documents association
Poorly controlled DMModerate More commonWorse with hyperglycemia
Evidence levelLow LimitedOnly 1 study confirmed association
Diabetes Type Association
Type 2 DM — More common Limited evidence (1 study)

Clinical Significance: Rubeosis faciei has limited evidence for DM association (only 1 article confirmed link). It can be confused with other causes of facial redness (rosacea, seborrheic dermatitis). Should not be used as a standalone screening marker but may support clinical suspicion in context.

12. Lipodystrophy (Lipohypertrophy & Lipoatrophy)

Insulin-Induced Localized Fat Disorders
Lipodystrophy — localized fat disorders at insulin injection sitesWikimedia Commons / CDC PHIL (Public Domain)

Description: Localized fat tissue changes at insulin injection sites. Lipohypertrophy (most common) presents as rubbery, firm swellings from repeated trauma and anabolic effects of insulin at the same site. Lipoatrophy (rarer) presents as depressed areas from immune-mediated destruction of fat. Both are caused by repeated insulin injections into the same location.

Prevalence
PopulationPrevalenceEvidence
Insulin-using patientsHigh ~27–50%Very common with poor injection technique
Type 1 DMHigh Very commonMost insulin-treated T1DM patients
LipohypertrophyHigh More commonUp to ~50% of insulin users
LipoatrophyLow RareLess common with modern insulin analogs
Diabetes Type Association
Type 1 DM — Primary Type 2 DM — Insulin-treated patients Insulin therapy-related

Clinical Significance: Lipodystrophy is a treatment-related complication of insulin therapy. Both types can affect insulin absorption unpredictably, causing glycemic variability. Prevention requires rotating injection sites. Switching to newer insulin analogs reduces risk of lipoatrophy.

  • Lipohypertrophy: most common (~27–50% of insulin users) — firm swellings at injection sites
  • Lipoatrophy: rarer — depressed areas from fat necrosis (less common with modern insulins)
  • Affects insulin absorption — can cause unexplained hypoglycemia
  • Prevention: rotate injection sites properly

13. Diabetic Foot Ulcers (DFU)

Neuropathic & Ischemic Ulceration — Major DM Complication
📷
Neuropathic Diabetic Foot Ulcer — heelWikimedia image unavailable (rate-limited)
Neuropathic diabetic foot ulcer — heel ulcer showing tissue loss due to peripheral neuropathyWikimedia Commons / Stevenfruitsmaak (CC Licensed)

Description: Full-thickness skin loss on the foot, typically due to a combination of peripheral neuropathy, peripheral arterial disease (ischemia), and infection. Ulcers are usually painless (due to neuropathy) and located on pressure points (plantar surface, toes, heels). DFUs are the leading cause of non-traumatic lower limb amputations worldwide. Healing is slow and often requires specialized wound care.

Prevalence

Lifetime risk in DMHigh ~15–25%Major diabetic complication
Annual incidenceHigh ~2–6%Of all diabetic patients per year
Amputation riskHigh ~1 in 6 DFU patientsLeading cause of non-traumatic amputation
Type 2 DMHigh More commonMore common in T2DM
Diabetes Type Association
Type 2 DM — More common Type 1 DM — Present Neuropathy & ischemia-related

Clinical Significance: DFUs represent the most serious dermatological complication of diabetes. Risk factors: neuropathy, ischemia, foot deformities, trauma. Prevention includes regular foot inspection, glycemic control, and appropriate footwear. Once ulcerated, multidisciplinary wound care is essential.

  • ~15–25% lifetime risk in diabetics — leading cause of non-traumatic amputation
  • Painless due to neuropathy — patients may not notice until advanced
  • Combination of neuropathy + ischemia + infection
  • Requires multidisciplinary wound care — podiatry, vascular surgery, infectious disease

15. Fungal Skin Infections in DM

Candidiasis, Dermatophytosis, Onychomycosis, Mucormycosis
🦠
Tinea PedisAthlete's foot — dermatophyte infection
Very common in diabetes (up to 50%)
Wikimedia Commons (CC Licensed)
Tinea pedis (athlete's foot) — dermatophyte infection very common in DM patientsWikimedia Commons / Klaus D. Peter (CC Licensed)

Description: Fungal infections are significantly more common and more severe in diabetic patients due to impaired immunity, hyperglycemia providing a favorable environment for fungal growth, and compromised skin integrity. Common presentations include Candida albicans infections (intertriginous candidiasis, angular cheilitis), dermatophytosis (tinea pedis, tinea corporis), onychomycosis (fungal nail infections), and serious opportunistic infections like mucormycosis.

Clinical Significance: Recurrent or severe fungal infections — especially candidiasis — should prompt diabetes screening. Mucormycosis is a rare but life-threatening opportunistic infection with high mortality in diabetics with DKA. Requires urgent surgical debridement and antifungal therapy.

  • Fungal infections are 2–3× more prevalent in diabetics vs general population
  • Candida infections (intertriginous, mucosal) are most common
  • Mucormycosis is rare but fatal — associated with DKA and ketoacidosis
  • Onychomycosis can serve as a portal of entry for bacterial superinfection

16. Acquired Reactive Perforating Collagenosis (ARPC)

Pruritic Hyperkeratotic Papules — Transepidermal Collagen Elimination
ARPC — pruritic hyperkeratotic papules with central keratin plug (representative image)Wikimedia Commons (CC Licensed) — representative image

Description: A rare cutaneous disorder characterized by pruritic, erythematous papules with a central keratin plug (hyperkeratotic center). Lesions are typically 2–8mm and appear on the extensor surfaces of the arms, legs, and trunk. ARPC is the most frequently reported perforating dermatosis in patients with diabetes mellitus and chronic renal insufficiency. Koebner phenomenon (trauma-induced) plays a role.

Prevalence

Chronic renal diseaseModerate Strong associationOften co-occurs with diabetic nephropathy
With pruritusHigh Very commonAlmost always presents with itching
RarityLow RareOverall uncommon condition
Diabetes Type Association
Type 2 DM — More common Chronic kidney disease co-association

Clinical Significance: ARPC is a marker of end-organ damage in diabetes — particularly diabetic nephropathy and chronic renal disease. The intense pruritus is difficult to manage. Topical retinoids, intralesional steroids, and phototherapy have been used with variable success.

  • Rare perforating dermatosis — almost always presents with severe pruritus
  • Strong association with diabetic nephropathy and chronic renal disease
  • Koebner phenomenon — can be triggered by scratching/trauma
  • Treatment is challenging — topical retinoids, steroids, phototherapy
